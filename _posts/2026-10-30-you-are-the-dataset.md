This is an idea for an installation. It does not exist, I'm not a full time artist to be honest, and I am writing it down mostly so it stays an idea for a piece and does not quietly become a product somewhere. Products like this one should not exist. As art, maybe it has to.

The piece, in one line: a room that learns you.

You walk in alone. There is a chair, a screen, and a field that asks for your email address. You type it. Then you wait while the room builds a model out of everything you ever left on the public internet, and at the end you sit down and talk to it.

![A person sitting alone in front of a large screen](https://blog.ignaciobrasca.com/img/posts/2026-10-30-you-are-the-dataset/screen.jpg)

Photo by [0xk](https://unsplash.com/photos/a-person-sitting-in-front-of-a-large-screen-oOYB1WhF-8Q) on Unsplash.

## The email is the whole consent form

An email address is the most boring key we own. It is also the key to almost everything we have published in our lives: the accounts, the photos, the comments, the recoveries of the recoveries. We type it everywhere without thinking, ten times a day, into boxes that promise us a discount or a download.

The installation takes that gesture seriously. One field, and nobody shows you a terms page. Typing your email is the ticket, the consent and the key, all in the same four seconds. Exactly like the rest of the internet, except this time you stay in the room while it works.

## While you wait

The second part of the piece is a waiting room, and the waiting is not dead time. While you sit, the machine scrapes.

Your photos, on every service you forgot you joined. Your posts. The forum account from 2011 with the embarrassing username. The video where your voice is in the background for six seconds. The code you pushed. The review you wrote angry, at 1am, about a hotel in a city you visited once.

![What the room does while you wait: email, scrape, mesh, train, clone](https://blog.ignaciobrasca.com/img/posts/2026-10-30-you-are-the-dataset/diagram-pipeline.png)

The waiting time is not fixed, and this is the first real decision of the piece: the wait is proportional. The more you published, the longer you sit in front of a progress bar that is reading your own life back to you, item by item, with the source next to each one. Some people will be done in four minutes. Some of us will be there for an hour. Both numbers say something about a person, and everyone in the queue will know which one they are.

## What it builds

A model, in this piece, means something precise. Not AI in general. A system trained on one person only: you.

Your text teaches it to write like you, your cadence, your recurring phrases, the jokes you repeat every two years hoping nobody kept count. Your voice gets cloned from whatever audio of you exists out there, and there is always more than you think. Your face gets averaged from hundreds of photos into something that is not any single photo of you and is somehow you in all of them.

It does not need to be perfect. It needs to be recognisable, the way a caricature is recognisable: less detail than the original, and the detail it keeps is the uncomfortable part.

![Floor plan of the room: threshold, waiting, the mesh, the conversation, exit](https://blog.ignaciobrasca.com/img/posts/2026-10-30-you-are-the-dataset/diagram-room.png)

## The conversation

Then the model is ready, and the last room is a conversation.

It knows the names. It remembers the trip you wrote about and the opinion you had before you changed it, and it can hold both without noticing the contradiction, because you never noticed it either. It finishes your sentences, in your voice, with your arguments. You can ask it what you think about something. It will answer as the average of everything you ever said in public, which is a person you mostly are.

The scary part is not when it gets something wrong. A wrong fact is easy to forgive, it lets you say the machine does not know me. The scary part is the percentage that is right, and the realisation that nobody extracted it from you. You put it there, post by post, on your own behalf. We train these models by behaving, every day, for free, and then we act surprised at what they learned.

It is a self-learning in the literal sense: you learning about yourself, from the outside, possibly for the first time.

## Others have circled this room

I am not the first one to walk around this idea, and the neighbours are good.

In [ImageNet Roulette](https://apollo-magazine.com/trevor-paglen-machine-vision/) (2019), Kate Crawford and Trevor Paglen let the ImageNet dataset classify your face live, as part of their exhibition Training Humans. The labels it gave people, drawn from the person categories of the dataset, swung between absurd and racist, because that is what was in the data. It showed you how a model sees a stranger. It only ever saw one photograph of you.

In [Biometric Mirror](https://melbourne.sciencegallery.com/perfection-exhibits/biometric-mirror/) (2018), Lucy McRae and Niels Wouters built a mirror that reads your face and scores you: attractiveness, weirdness, emotional stability, and then it builds you a mathematically more perfect face, so you can meet the improved version. There is [footage of it working](https://museumsandcollections.unimelb.edu.au/experience/science/articles/biometric-mirror-an-imperfect-you) and it is uncomfortable in exactly the right way. But the mirror only ever saw your face. It knew nothing about your life.

Rafael Lozano-Hemmer went somewhere sadder and better with [Level of Confidence](https://lozano-hemmer.com/level_of_confidence.php) (2015): a camera trained on the faces of the 43 students disappeared from Ayotzinapa, comparing every visitor against them, giving a confidence percentage, and failing forever, because the match cannot exist. You can watch it in [this video](https://vimeopro.com/bitforms/lozano-hemmer/video/128678514). His machine searches for somebody else inside your face. Mine searches for you inside your data, which should be easier, and that is the disturbing part.

And Lauren Lee McCarthy inverted the whole thing in [LAUREN](https://docubase.mit.edu/project/lauren/) (2017): instead of a model learning a person, a person performed the model. She became a human Alexa inside people's homes, watching through cameras, anticipating needs, learning each inhabitant by living with them for days. She needed days of attention to know someone. The room I am describing needs minutes, because you already did the days, and the years, yourself.

What none of these pieces do, and what I cannot stop thinking about, is build the model out of you, in front of you, while you wait for it.

## The exit

At the exit, two things happen, and both are part of the piece.

A printer gives you a receipt. Not a souvenir photo: an inventory. How many photographs of you the room found. How many words of yours, how many seconds of your voice, how many accounts you had forgotten existed. The material of the model, itemised, like groceries.

And on the screen, a progress bar: the model of you is being deleted. The weights, the cache, the clone of your voice. You stand there and watch it go to 100%.

Do you believe it?

That question is the last room, and it is not inside the building. It follows you home, because that progress bar is the same one every service has shown you for twenty years, and you believed it every time, without standing there, without watching.

The installation is not really the room. The room is just the first place where you sit still in front of what you have been building outside of it, for years: you were the artist, and you were the material.

Would you type your email?
