I've been working in a heavy agentic workflow lately, and I foresee two states of what we are doing.

Barebones: enough architecture for us as a species to extract data and act on behalf of the commander.

And on the other side: problems utterly complex to scrape from.

The first to fall will be authentication. If everything is open, there is no one left to prove yourself to.

Then the web. Boxes in the shadow, communicating through obscure protocols optimized for machines, not us.

There will be no network but a market. It will be there for us to consume from, a GPS of knowledge, a universal standard. No need to connect yourself to it. You will already be there.

Not your name, not your identity. A rasterization of all your bits of information, chained together in a sequence only you have.

Decision makers will not look at landing pages. Insights will be exposed directly, for autonomous pointers, in a `.txt`. Like `llms.txt`, but `insights.txt`: readable as data, not as content or usability.

It's the end of human-interface. We don't need to know.

![Paul Dirac at the blackboard](https://blog.ignaciobrasca.com/img/posts/2026-10-10-we-dont-need-to-know/dirac.jpg)

As Dirac said when his formula got published:

> "It seems to be one of the fundamental features of nature that fundamental physical laws are described in terms of a mathematical theory of great beauty and power, needing quite a high standard of mathematics for one to understand it."

We want to be there. We want to be present in the loop, part of the node that orchestrates the rest. But we are being displaced from the conversation.

We should take the opportunity: think outside the box, and take on better problems we can finally tackle.

We won't go back. Not unless energy and infrastructure logistics, turbines, steam engines, all of it, are not sufficient.

If I had a billion dollars right now, I would invest in elevators, fire and maintenance, HVAC.
