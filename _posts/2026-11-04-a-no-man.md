These days I'm wondering what would be the most effective way to communicate in an organization [where almost everyone is its own manager](https://proton.me/blog/ai-manifesto). Julia Evans covers this in depth in [Help! I Have a Manager!](https://wizardzines.com/zines/manager), also how to work successfully in a team, although there's something bittersweet nowadays about being part of a faster and robust team.

Bold ideas can take place, although for the sake of performative visibility, might be the only ones at all.

How to work successfully in your role is part of oneself, although a thought process will be: how to be successfully good in an organization without abusing the rules?

Photo by [mark wang](https://unsplash.com/photos/evergreen-tree-in-snowy-landscape-8crWv7cNSe4) on Unsplash.

[Playing the game in the shadows](https://lalitm.com/software-engineering-outside-the-spotlight/) doesn't really play well either…

So, knowing an organization is [its own worst enemy](http://www.shirky.com/writings/group_enemy.html), we should wonder if there's a way to interact with an organization without being part of the same system that makes it collapse or put it to sleep.

If we treat an organization, a group of people, as an organism, one can consider this set (since we cannot repeat ourselves) as an array of decisions and each of these decisions, let's call it: human synapses, can interact with others and if one is repeated, many times, these paths, or bridges, can materialize and make it incredibly easy to proceed always using this path instead of creating new ones.

Probably this is the reason why games of power inside an organization are always being played out by always the same specific individuals.

Now, let's think of it as when you play a board game: you can stick to the rules and no one will say anything, just as you can play with your own modified patches and no one will call the game designers and mention it. Why? What changes between this and cheating in a game online? Did you ever reason about it?

I'll say the same is true within an organization: whoever is successful inside will be perishing all the negative aspects of it (see [The Gervais Principle](https://ribbonfarm.com/2009/10/07/the-gervais-principle-or-the-office-according-to-the-office)). This was mentioned in many articles before this one, although in here I want to comment on one thing that will make you reliable, robust and safe in an institution: learn to say no.

Elements in a system shift towards optimization of the consumption of energy.

Optimization is a universal problem everyone else is trying to resolve as we speak. Organism. Events. Networks.

However now a question, is a **network** an organization with self conscience? In the concept of an [extended mind](https://fitelson.org/prosem/clark_chalmers.pdf), cognition will always leak into notebooks, documents, any kind of artifact the organization is trying to produce. The [thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_mind_thesis) plants the idea of the mind as an entity outside our own which extends into the physical world. Your identity inside your workplace is not __you__, it's what the workplace needs __from you__.

Ideally, at this point we learn the system is broken, because the whole concept of a different scenario makes us realize it might not be working as expected, although it's perfectly functional. Once an individual interacting with it learns immediately how to traverse around it, moving one bit of information after the other until it settles, **it simply works**; the situation improves around him, the network gets stable, the rest won't matter much. The whole goal of a node in this network is to be efficient with it, not to achieve a goal.

You won't need much if you develop this craft and yeah, yeah, I know, it's easier to say than to act on, but hear me out... saying no is an art.

List and Pettit claimed something interesting on this topic in *Group Agency*, picked up in [a recent paper on collective agency](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10727953/): once an organization has a constitution, rules for turning member inputs into group judgments, it can hold beliefs and goals that contradict what most of its members believe, and act on them consistently. A court, a company, a party. Those judgments belong to the group, not to anyone in it. That is a group mind in the functional sense, not as a metaphor.

Organizations nowadays behave as people, because they technically are before the law, despite responsibilities and a few other things (see [The Corporation](https://cfe.tiff.net/canadianfilmencyclopedia/content/films/corporation), documentary, 2003), although we teach them how to be a psychopath child with no respect for others, no learning, no self-awareness, big egos, and more.

If we do an analogy to all what we have been saying, saying no is basically protecting your neurons from settling, from being part of a much more organized consensus. Saying no will make you an individual that cares.

However, having said all of that, you should never say no as a way to defend yourself or avoid being part of __the bigger thing__, or even to showcase that you know it won't work. You should say no because there's no other way around other than to say yes.

The nodes speak into each other explaining ways to save energy.

Going back to being a no-man, it is a practical alternative of this, putting the edges on whatever you are doing and with whoever you are working with. No means of respect, power and even intelligence. Means of social transportation.

I __was__ a yes-man, which will buy you time against a lot of adversities, although being a yes-man means that you will never be able to start to be a no-man, otherwise the organization will see __the change__, an allergic reaction the organism is not prepared for.
